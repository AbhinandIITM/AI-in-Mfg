\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem Statement}
CNC face milling is a fundamental manufacturing operation where a rotating cutter removes material from a workpiece in multiple passes. The cutting parameters -- spindle speed ($n$), feed per revolution ($f_r$), and axial depth of cut ($a_p$) -- must be chosen to balance four competing objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Productivity:} Maximize material removal rate ($\MRR = v_c \cdot a_p \cdot f_r$)
    \item \textbf{Energy Efficiency:} Minimize total electrical energy consumption ($E_{\text{total}}$), accounting for both machine idle power and cutting power.
    \item \textbf{Surface Quality Assurance:} Strictly adhere to finished workpiece roughness requirements ($\Ra \le 1.6\ \mu\text{m}$).
    \item \textbf{Tool Wear and Breakage Avoidance:} Limit cumulative flank wear ($\VB \le 0.3\ \text{mm}$) and mitigate catastrophic tool failure risks.
\end{enumerate}

Traditional approaches use fixed parameters from handbooks or static optimization (e.g., NSGA-II) that pick one parameter set for the entire batch. These cannot adapt to the \textit{state} of the process -- tool wear accumulates, workpiece geometry changes, and material properties vary between batches.

\subsection{Proposed Approach}
This project develops a \textbf{Digital Twin} of the milling process -- a high-fidelity physics-based simulator grounded in empirical manufacturing literature -- and trains a \textbf{Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)} agent to dynamically optimize cutting parameters pass-by-pass. The agent observes the current state (flank wear, remaining stock, previous parameters) together with the context it is optimizing for (the energy weight and the material), and selects the next pass parameters to maximize a reward that balances throughput and energy while respecting constraints.

Key innovations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speed-dependent specific cutting energy} $k_c(v_c) = k_{c0} (v_c/v_\text{ref})^m$ creates a genuine trade-off: faster cutting increases energy per unit volume but reduces idle energy.
    \item \textbf{Domain randomization} during training: material/tool coefficients, workpiece geometry, and initial wear are sampled per episode from literature-calibrated ranges.
    \item \textbf{One environment, one policy:} The energy weight $w_2$ and the material context live in the observation, so a single PPO policy covers the entire Pareto frontier and a whole distribution of materials.
    \item \textbf{Transfer experiment:} Measures how much a policy trained on one material knows about a harder, faster-wearing material.
    \item \textbf{Receding-horizon controller baseline:} A strong classical benchmark that re-solves at every pass.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A Gymnasium-compliant milling environment with explicit physics, energy-aware reward, and domain randomization.
    \item A trained PPO policy evaluated via 100-scenario Monte Carlo sweep with 95\% confidence intervals.
    \item Energy-weight ablation study (7 weights $\times$ 3 seeds) mapping the empirical Pareto frontier.
    \item Comparison against 5 classical baselines, all on the same episodes:\\
    Constant-High, Constant-Safe, Rule-Based, NSGA-II Static, Receding-Horizon.
    \item Transfer experiment on a harder alloy: a policy trained across a distribution of materials needs no adaptation there, while a policy trained on the nominal material violates the roughness limit until it is fine tuned.
    \item Interactive Streamlit workstation with 2D stock depletion, micro-topography, G-code generation, and multi-controller comparison.
    \item Full reproducibility: all results regenerated from committed artifacts by\\
    \texttt{experiments/summarize\_results.py}.
\end{enumerate}

Both reference papers treat energy as related work rather than as an optimization target: in each of them energy appears in the discussion of prior art, not among the objectives. Mo et al. optimize the surface roughness and the production time of a multi-pass turning operation and carry the tool condition and the tool damage in the decision variables, so the wear and the roughness marks carry over. Wang et al. (2025a) build their state from sequences of machining parameters and cutting force features, and their objectives from the dimensional error of the machining features and the machining time; neither the tool wear nor the roughness enters their formulation, so only the dynamic parameter optimization carries over. The third paper in \texttt{docs/papers/} (Wang et al., 2025b) solves the discrete step sequencing problem, so continuous cutting parameters are outside its scope.

The speed-dependent cutting energy model (Section~\ref{sec:physics}) is the key enabler here: without it the energy of a part is fixed by the volume removed, both objectives point the same way, and no weight on the energy term can open a trade-off. Putting the energy weight and the material context in the observation reduces the ablation cost from 21 training runs to one. The receding-horizon controller provides a fair classical benchmark that uses the same physics and re-optimizes at every pass.

% 3. DIGITAL TWIN PHYSICS
% ========================================================================
\section{Digital Twin Physics}
\label{sec:physics}

All equations are implemented in \texttt{MillingDT/config/physics.py} and used by the environment, baselines, simulator, and evaluation scripts -- a single source of truth. The state the agent sees is the 11-dimensional observation of Section~\ref{sec:obs}: eight physics features followed by the conditioning context, and the context is present in every task, so the equations below are the same equations for every task.

\subsection{Geometry and Kinematics}
Cutter diameter $D = 50\ \text{mm}$, cutting length $L_\text{cut} = 100\ \text{mm}$, total machining allowance $\kappa_\text{total} = 5.0\ \text{mm}$.
\begin{align}
    v_c &= \frac{\pi D n}{1000} \quad &&\text{[m/min]} \label{eq:vc} \\
    \MRR &= v_c \cdot a_p \cdot f_r \quad &&\text{[mm}^3/\text{min]} \label{eq:mrr} \\
    t_\text{cut} &= \frac{L_\text{cut}}{n f_r} \quad &&\text{[min]} \label{eq:tcut} \\
    N_\text{passes} &= \left\lceil \frac{\kappa_\text{total}}{a_p} \right\rceil \quad &&\text{[integer]} \label{eq:npasses}
\end{align}

\subsection{Tool Wear (Extended Taylor Model)}
\label{sec:wear}
Flank wear rate per pass:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \VB = C_\text{wear} \cdot v_c^{\alpha} \cdot f_r^{\beta} \cdot a_p^{\gamma_\text{wear}} \cdot t_\text{cut}
    \label{eq:wear}
\end{equation}
with calibrated coefficients $C_\text{wear}=1.1\times10^{-4}$, $\alpha=0.3$, $\beta=0.2$, $\gamma_\text{wear}=0.15$. Cumulative wear $V_{B,k} = \sum_{i=1}^k \Delta V_{B,i}$.

\subsection{Surface Roughness (Empirical Regression)}
\label{sec:roughness}
\begin{equation}
    \Ra = k_1 f_r^2 + k_2 \VB + \frac{k_3}{v_c}
    \label{eq:roughness}
\end{equation}
with $k_1=12.5$, $k_2=0.8$, $k_3=0.5$. The finished surface (last pass) must satisfy $\Ra \le \RaMax = 1.6\ \mu\text{m}$.

\subsection{Energy Consumption}
\label{sec:energy}
Specific cutting energy grows with cutting speed:
\begin{equation}
    k_c(v_c) = k_{c0} \left( \frac{v_c}{v_\text{ref}} \right)^m,
    \quad P_\text{cut} = k_c(v_c) \cdot \MRR,
    \quad P_\text{total} = P_\text{idle} + P_\text{cut}
    \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
with $P_\text{idle}=500\ \text{W}$, $k_{c0}=2.5\ \text{W}\cdot\text{min/mm}^3$, $m=0.6$, $v_\text{ref}$ from the reference cutting condition.
Energy per pass: $E_\text{pass} = P_\text{total} \cdot t_\text{cut}$.
Total energy per part: $E_\text{part} = \sum_{k=1}^{N_\text{passes}} E_{\text{pass},k}$.

\begin{proposition}[Interior Minimum]
With $m>0$, the energy per part $E_\text{part}(v_c)$ has an interior minimum in $v_c$. The cutting term rises with $v_c$ while the idle term falls, creating a genuine trade-off that the energy weight $w_2$ can select.
\end{proposition}
This is validated by test \texttt{test\_part\_energy\_has\_an\_interior\_minimum\_in\_speed}.

\subsection{Stochastic Tool Breakage}
Probability of catastrophic breakage per pass, applied only once the flank wear passes a threshold:
\begin{equation}
    p_\text{break} = 0.3 \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\VB - 0.15}{\VBMax - 0.15}}_{\text{wear factor}} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{\MRR}{\MRR_\text{max}}\right)^2}_{\text{load factor}}
    \qquad (\VB > 0.15\ \text{mm},\ \text{else } 0)
\end{equation}
where $\MRR_\text{max}$ is the removal rate of the machine envelope at its upper bounds. The draw comes from the environment's own generator, which \texttt{reset(seed=...)} seeds, so a scenario replayed with the same seed breaks in the same place. If triggered, the episode terminates with a $-100$ reward.

\section{Environment Design}

\subsection{Registrations}
One Gymnasium environment is registered in \texttt{MillingDT/\allowbreak env/\_\_init\_\_.py}, and it is exported as the constant \texttt{MillingDT.env.ENV\_ID}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \texttt{MillingDTEnv-v1}: the only registration. The observation is always $\R^{11}$: the eight physics features followed by the conditioning context (the energy weight $w_2$, the wear coefficient $C_\text{wear}$ and the roughness coefficient $k_1$). The flags \texttt{randomize} and \texttt{randomize\_weight} decide whether the scenario and the energy weight are drawn per \texttt{reset()}. A task with a pinned weight is the same environment with a constant in the context, so there is a single observation shape across the whole project.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{State Space}
\label{sec:obs}
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Observation (11 dimensions), every feature mapped onto $[0,1]$}
\label{tab:obs}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Index} & \textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Normalization} \\
\midrule
0 & Flank wear $\VB$ & $\tanh$ squash over $[0, \VBMax]$ \\
1 & Remaining stock $\kappa_\text{rem}$ & $\tanh$ squash over $[0, \kappa_\text{total}]$ \\
2 & Previous spindle speed $n$ & $(n-n_\text{min})/(n_\text{max}-n_\text{min})$ \\
3 & Previous feed $f_r$ & $(f_r-f_\text{min})/(f_\text{max}-f_\text{min})$ \\
4 & Previous depth $a_p$ & $(a_p-a_{p,\text{min}})/(a_{p,\text{max}}-a_{p,\text{min}})$ \\
5 & Previous $\MRR$ & $\MRR / \MRR_\text{max}$, clipped \\
6 & Previous roughness $\Ra$ & $\tanh$ squash over $[0, \RaMax]$ \\
7 & Tool broken & 1 if broken, else 0 \\
8 & Energy weight $w_2$ & $w_2 \in [0,1]$ \\
9 & Wear coefficient $C_\text{wear}$ & over the scenario band $[0.6, 1.4]$ of nominal \\
10 & Roughness coefficient $k_1$ & over the scenario band $[0.8, 1.2]$ of nominal \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Every feature is normalized against the \emph{scenario's own} limits, so the observation is scale free across geometries and materials. The $\tanh$ squashing keeps the gradient magnitude when a constraint is violated, instead of clipping the value to 1.

The context occupies the last three slots in every task. A policy trained with a pinned weight sees a constant there, a policy trained with the weight drawn sees the weight it is being asked for, and a policy trained across the material distribution sees the material it is cutting. The shape never changes, so one trained policy can be asked for any weight and any material at runtime.

\subsection{Action Space}
Continuous 3D box $\mathcal{A} = [-1, 1]^3$, mapped to physical parameters:
\begin{align}
    n &= n_\text{min} + \frac{a_1+1}{2}(n_\text{max}-n_\text{min}) \\
    f_r &= f_\text{min} + \frac{a_2+1}{2}(f_\text{max}-f_\text{min}) \\
    a_p &= a_{p,\text{min}} + \frac{a_3+1}{2}(a_{p,\text{max}}-a_{p,\text{min}})
\end{align}
The policy output is unbounded; the environment clips to $[-1,1]$ before mapping, guaranteeing the machine envelope is never violated.

\subsection{Reward Function}
The per-step reward is:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
r ={}& \underbrace{5\left(w_1 \frac{\MRR}{\MRR_\text{max}} - w_2 \frac{E_\text{pass}}{E_\text{ref}}\right)}_{\text{Objective}}
+ \underbrace{2\,\Delta\!\left(\tfrac{\kappa_\text{removed}}{\kappa_\text{total}}\right)}_{\text{Progress}} \\
& - \underbrace{50\,\mathbb{1}[\VB > \VBSafe]}_{\text{Wear penalty}}
- \underbrace{100\,\mathbb{1}[\text{broken}]}_{\text{Breakage}} \\
& - \underbrace{50\,\mathbb{1}[\text{finishing pass} \wedge \Ra > \RaSafe]}_{\text{Roughness penalty}}
- 0.05
\end{split}
\end{equation}
plus, on a part finished inside spec, a $+200$ completion bonus and a Pareto margin bonus of at most $+5$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{HV} = \max\!\left(0, 1 - \tfrac{T_\text{total}}{T_\text{ref}}\right) \cdot \max\!\left(0, 1 - \tfrac{\Ra}{\RaSafe}\right) \cdot 5
\end{equation}

The objective term carries a scale factor of 5 so that it competes with the shaping terms. Without it the progress bonus and the constraint penalties decide the policy on their own, and the energy weight $w_2$ has no visible effect at all: an earlier revision of the reward produced 21 identical ablation policies. The constraint penalties and the completion bonus are an order of magnitude larger on purpose, so that breaking a constraint is never worth a throughput or energy gain. $\RaSafe = 0.85\,\RaMax$, $w_1 + w_2 = 1$, and $E_\text{ref}$ is the energy of a pass under the reference condition, recomputed for every scenario from that scenario's diameter, cutting length and energy coefficients.

The reward is validated by \texttt{test\_reward\_matches\_an\_independent\_calculation}, which recomputes it from \texttt{MillingDT/config/physics.py}, independently of the code that assembles it, and asserts agreement to $10^{-9}$.

\subsection{Episode Logic}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{terminated=True}: Part finished ($\kappa_\text{rem} \le 0$), tool broken, or wear exceeded.
    \item \texttt{truncated=True}: Maximum steps (200) reached.
    \item The roughness penalty is charged once, at the finishing pass, and only when that pass ends outside $\RaSafe$. Intermediate passes carry no roughness penalty; the wear penalty is charged on every pass whose cumulative flank wear exceeds $\VBSafe$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Domain Randomization}
When \texttt{randomize=True}, each \texttt{reset()} samples a new scenario from \texttt{MillingDT/\allowbreak config/\allowbreak scenarios.py}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Workpiece: $\kappa_\text{total} \in [2, 15]\ \text{mm}$, $D \in [40, 150]\ \text{mm}$, $L_\text{cut} \in [50, 200]\ \text{mm}$
    \item Material/Tool: $C_\text{wear}$ at $[0.6, 1.4]\times$ nominal, $k_1, k_2, k_3$ each at $[0.8, 1.2]\times$ nominal
    \item Initial wear: $V_{B,0} \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 0.8]\,\VBMax$
\end{itemize}
The same tables are used for Monte Carlo evaluation, so the policy is measured on the distribution it was trained on. Measured on the trained policy, the eight physics features the policy receives on the two sets agree to 0.041 in the mean of their normalized values, against a standard deviation of 1.

\section{Reinforcement Learning}

\subsection{Algorithm}
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with MLP policy, implemented via Stable-Baselines3.
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{PPO hyperparameters}
\label{tab:ppo}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
Policy & MlpPolicy (2 hidden layers, 256 units each) \\
Learning rate & $3 \times 10^{-4}$ (linear schedule to 0) \\
$n\_steps$ & 2048 \\
Batch size & 64 \\
$n\_epochs$ & 10 \\
$\gamma$ & 0.99 \\
GAE $\lambda$ & 0.95 \\
Clip range & 0.2 \\
Entropy coefficient & 0.02 \\
Value function coefficient & 0.5 \\
Max grad norm & 0.5 \\
VecNormalize & \texttt{norm\_obs=True, norm\_reward=True} \\
Environments & 4 parallel (\texttt{SubprocVecEnv}) \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Training uses a custom \texttt{ProgressQueueCallback} that sends a heartbeat every 1000 steps to the supervisor (\texttt{run\_experiments.py}); a dropped heartbeat triggers worker termination.

\subsection{Training Runs}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Production policy:} 1,000,000 steps, seed 42, \texttt{--randomize --randomize-weight}. Both the machining scenario and the energy weight are drawn per episode, so one network covers the whole weight range and the whole material distribution. Model at\\
    \texttt{results/run\_PPO\_seed42/}. This is the policy the dashboard, the Monte Carlo sweep, the runtime weight sweep and the baseline comparison all use.
    \item \textbf{Ablation grid:} 7 weights $w_2 \in \{0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0\} \times$ 3 seeds $\{42,43,44\}$, 150,000 steps each, trained in parallel via \texttt{experiments/run\_experiments.py}. Every ablation policy is trained with its energy weight \textbf{pinned} and is evaluated at the weight it was trained for, so it is always asked the question it was trained for. The model directories follow the pattern \texttt{results/run\_ablation\_w2\_0\_4\_seed42}, one per weight and seed.
\end{enumerate}

Both training paths use the same script, \texttt{experiments/train\_rl.py}: \texttt{--randomize} draws a new machining scenario per episode, \texttt{--randomize-weight} draws a new energy weight per episode, and \texttt{--w2} pins the weight of the task.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Evaluation}
\texttt{python -m experiments.evaluate --scenarios 100} runs the production policy over 100 randomized scenarios (fixed seed 42). Each scenario is a full machining episode. Reports mean $\pm$ std and 95\% CI (t-distribution) for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\MRR$, Energy/part, Cycle time
    \item Max $\Ra$, Max $\VB$
    \item Constraint violation rates (worst pass vs finished surface)
    \item Tool breakage rate, Part completion rate
\end{itemize}

\section{Classical Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

Five non-RL baselines implemented in \texttt{experiments/run\_baselines.py}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Constant-High:} $n=8000, f_r=0.35, a_p=2.5$ (upper bounds).
    \item \textbf{Constant-Safe:} $n=2000, f_r=0.05, a_p=0.1$ (lower bounds).
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based Degradation:} Feed at maximum, spindle speed reduced by 20\% every second pass, as a shop-floor heuristic for tool degradation.
    \item \textbf{NSGA-II Static:} Multi-objective optimization (pymoo) picking \textit{one} parameter set for the whole part. Decision variables $[n, f_r, a_p]$. Objectives: maximize $\MRR$, minimize Energy. Constraints: $\RaFinal \le \RaMax$, $V_{B,\text{final}} \le \VBMax$.
    \item \textbf{Receding-Horizon Controller:} Vectorized grid search over $(n, f_r, a_p)$ at \textit{every pass}, simulating the remaining passes with the current $\VB$ and $\kappa_\text{rem}$. Keeps feasible candidates, picks the best by the same scalarization $w_1 \MRR - w_2 E$. This is the strongest classical baseline.
\end{enumerate}

All baselines use the \textit{same physics} (\texttt{MillingDT/config/physics.py}) and are evaluated on the same 50 episodes of the nominal machining condition, at the same energy weight $w_2 = 0.4$ at which the learned policy is queried.

\subsection{Controller Initialization Experiment}
\label{sec:controllerinit}

The baseline comparison left one question open: the controller re-solves the next pass with the exact state and the exact physics, and the learned policy is level with it on the reward but behind it on the physical metrics. The experiment asks whether a policy that starts from the controller ends up better than the expert it started from.

The method is a behavior cloning step used as an initialization. The controller's behavior is recorded on 2000 randomized episodes, a supervised actor with the same shape as the PPO actor (two hidden layers of 256 with Tanh) is fitted on those trajectories by regression, and the fitted weights are copied into a fresh PPO policy. The reinforcement learning therefore starts from a feasible behavior instead of from noise. The policy is then trained with the same budget as the production policy (1,000,000 steps) and evaluated on the same 100 randomized scenarios at the same energy weight $w_2 = 0.4$. The clone lives in \texttt{MillingDT/\allowbreak agent/\allowbreak controller\_init.py}, the experiment in \texttt{experiments/\allowbreak controller\_experiment.py}, and the trained policy in \texttt{results/\allowbreak run\_PPO\_controller\_init\_seed42/}.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What Worked}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The speed-dependent cutting energy model ($k_c \propto v_c^m$) is essential. In an earlier revision of the reward, without it the energy/throughput trade-off vanished and the 21 ablation policies came out identical.
    \item Domain randomization + shared scenario tables: the policy generalizes to unseen scenarios (99\% part completion, 0\% finished-surface violations).
    \item Putting the energy weight in the observation reduces the ablation cost from 21 training runs to one, and the one policy beats the 21 per-weight policies at every weight.
    \item Training across a distribution of materials removes the need for an adaptation step: on a harder alloy the production policy is the best of the four transfer arms zero shot, while a policy trained on the nominal material violates the roughness limit until it is fine tuned.
    \item The receding-horizon controller is a strong, fair baseline using the same physics. It also exposed that the earlier baseline comparison was hollow.
    \item Initializing the policy from the controller does not make it beat the controller, but it does make it uniform: the reward std over the 100 scenarios falls from 30.0 to 3.4, the tool breakage falls to zero and every part is completed (Table~\ref{tab:controllerinit}).
    \item The test suite catches physics regressions (energy interior minimum, cutting energy speed dependence) and reward regressions (golden-value test).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The learned policy does not outperform the receding-horizon controller on the reward (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}), and the controller is ahead on the physical metrics. The controller re-solves with perfect physics; the policy has to learn the dynamics from experience. Initializing the policy from the controller does not close that gap: the initialized policy is more consistent (reward std 3.4 against 30.0) but slower and more hungry for energy (6.6\% less removal rate and 12.9\% more energy per part against the from-scratch policy), and the difference in mean reward, 199.35 against 198.57, is inside the standard error of the mean of 3.0.
    \item The energy weight ablation uses 150,000 steps per weight against 1,000,000 for the production policy, so the ablation frontier is a lower bound on what a pinned policy can reach and may shift with more training.
    \item The reward charges the energy of a pass, while the reported metric is the energy of a part. A part cut in many shallow passes draws less power per pass but spends more time on the idle machine, so the two can move in opposite directions. Both are reported.
    \item Stochastic tool breakage (1\% rate) introduces variance; the reward penalty could be shaped more carefully.
    \item The micro-topography model is a simplified feed-mark cusp; real chatter and vibration are not modeled.
    \item The energy model is the modelling choice of this work, not a result taken from the reference papers, and it is calibrated against a single reference condition.
    \item The frontier is symmetric but shallow: 8.4\% less removal rate buys 9.5\% less energy. A decision-maker who needs a large energy reduction has to pay for it in throughput elsewhere, and this digital twin does not yet show a way to escape that trade-off.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Work}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Against a restricted controller:} The controller re-solves every pass with the exact state and the exact physics, so it is an oracle that a learned policy cannot match from its observation alone. Restricting the controller's information, or pricing the cycle time explicitly in the reward, would tell whether the remaining gap is an information gap or an optimization gap.
    \item \textbf{Sim-to-real:} Calibrate physics coefficients on real machine data; add sensor noise and delay to the environment.
    \item \textbf{Multi-objective RL:} Replace scalarization with Pareto-conditioned networks or hypervolume maximization.
    \item \textbf{Runtime energy budget:} Drive the production policy with an optimizer that sets $w_2$ from an energy budget. The weight is a query, not a retrain.
    \item \textbf{Full 3D Digital Twin:} Extend the 2D cross-section to 3D toolpath visualization with collision checking.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This project demonstrates a complete pipeline for DRL-based CNC milling optimization:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A physics-grounded Digital Twin with speed-dependent cutting energy, tool wear, surface roughness, and stochastic breakage.
    \item A Gymnasium environment with domain randomization, an always 11-dimensional conditioned observation, and an energy-aware reward whose objective terms are scaled to compete with the shaping terms.
    \item PPO policies trained with proper experimental rigor: Monte Carlo evaluation with confidence intervals, an ablation study mapping the empirical Pareto frontier, and one production policy covering the frontier in a single run.
    \item Strong classical baselines including a receding-horizon controller that re-optimizes at every pass.
    \item A controller initialization experiment, which records the receding-horizon controller on 2000 episodes, clones its actor into a fresh PPO policy and trains it with the same 1,000,000-step budget. The initialized policy is much more uniform (reward std 3.4 against 30.0 over the 100 scenarios), breaks no tools and completes every part, but it does not beat the controller: the controller removes 38\% more material per minute and finishes in 13.5 s against 32.3 s.
    \item A transfer experiment on a harder alloy, which finds that a policy trained across a distribution of materials needs no adaptation there, while a policy trained on the nominal material violates the roughness limit until it is fine tuned.
    \item An interactive workstation for visualization, G-code generation, and multi-controller comparison.
    \item Full reproducibility: every result table is generated from committed artifacts by a single script.
\end{enumerate}

The key scientific finding is that \textbf{the speed-dependent specific cutting energy creates a genuine energy/throughput trade-off}, and a DRL agent can learn to navigate it pass-by-pass while respecting surface quality and tool life constraints. Across the measured frontier the removal rate falls 8.4\% while the energy of the part falls 9.5\%, a symmetric trade-off, and the spindle speed falls with the energy weight, which is the mechanism the model predicts. One policy with the weight in its observation makes the whole trade-off accessible at runtime, and it beats the 21 per-weight policies at every point of it.

The result on the classical side is the opposite of an advantage: the receding-horizon controller still removes more material per minute and finishes sooner than the learned policy, and initializing the policy from it does not change that. What the initialization does buy is consistency, and it buys it with throughput and energy. The claim this work supports is therefore that the reinforcement learning agent navigates the energy/throughput trade-off within a fixed constraint set, not that it beats a classical controller that re-optimizes with perfect physics.

\section{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}
\label{app:sensitivity}

The PPO hyperparameters in Table~\ref{tab:ppo} are the Stable-Baselines3 defaults, with three exceptions: the network is widened to $2\times256$ units, the entropy coefficient is raised to 0.02 to keep the policy from collapsing to deterministic actions early, and the training runs on the CPU with one thread per process so that several policies can train in parallel on one machine. Measured on this machine, PPO on this MLP runs at 812 steps/s on the CPU against 318 steps/s on the GPU, so the GPU is not used.

The entropy coefficient matters most: with too little of it the policy settles into a deterministic action early and stops exploring the envelope.

\section{Energy Model Derivation}
\label{app:energy}

The specific cutting energy $k_c(v_c)$ models the empirical observation that the energy required to remove a unit volume of material increases with cutting speed due to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Higher shear zone temperatures $\to$ increased flow stress
    \item Increased strain rates $\to$ strain-rate hardening
    \item Tool-chip interface friction changes
\end{enumerate}

The power-law form $k_c(v_c) = k_{c0} (v_c/v_\text{ref})^m$ with $m \approx 0.6$ is a standard empirical form for the rise of the specific cutting energy with cutting speed, and it is the modelling choice of this work rather than a result taken from either reference paper: the reference papers optimize quality and efficiency, and treat energy only as related work. The reference condition $v_\text{ref}$ is the cutting speed at the calibrated nominal parameters ($n=5000$ RPM, $D=50$ mm $\to v_\text{ref} \approx 785$ m/min). At this speed, $k_c = k_{c0} = 2.5$ W$\cdot$min/mm$^3$.

Total power: $P_\text{total} = P_\text{idle} + k_c(v_c) \cdot \MRR$.
Energy per pass: $E_\text{pass} = P_\text{total} \cdot t_\text{cut}$.
Substituting $t_\text{cut} = L_\text{cut} / (n f_r)$ and $\MRR = v_c a_p f_r$:
\begin{align}
    E_\text{pass} &= P_\text{idle} \frac{L_\text{cut}}{n f_r}
    + k_{c0} \left( \frac{v_c}{v_\text{ref}} \right)^m v_c a_p f_r \frac{L_\text{cut}}{n f_r} \\
    &= \underbrace{P_\text{idle} \frac{L_\text{cut}}{n f_r}}_\text{Idle energy}
    + \underbrace{k_{c0} \frac{L_\text{cut} a_p}{n f_r} \left( \frac{\pi D n}{1000 v_\text{ref}} \right)^m \frac{\pi D n}{1000}}_\text{Cutting energy}
\end{align}

The idle term $\propto 1/(n f_r)$ decreases with speed; the cutting term $\propto n^m$ increases with speed. Their sum has an interior minimum, which is the operating point selected by $w_2$.
