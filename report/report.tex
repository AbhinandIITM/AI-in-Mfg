\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem Statement}
CNC face milling is a fundamental manufacturing operation where a rotating cutter removes material from a workpiece in multiple passes. The cutting parameters -- spindle speed ($n$), feed per revolution ($f_r$), and axial depth of cut ($a_p$) -- must be chosen to balance four competing objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Productivity:} Maximize material removal rate ($\MRR = v_c \cdot a_p \cdot f_r$)
    \item \textbf{Energy Efficiency:} Minimize total electrical energy consumption ($E_{\text{total}}$), accounting for both machine idle power and cutting power.
    \item \textbf{Surface Quality Assurance:} Strictly adhere to finished workpiece roughness requirements ($\Ra \le 1.6\ \mu\text{m}$).
    \item \textbf{Tool Wear and Breakage Avoidance:} Limit cumulative flank wear ($\VB \le 0.3\ \text{mm}$) and mitigate catastrophic tool failure risks.
\end{enumerate}

Traditional approaches use fixed parameters from handbooks or static optimization (e.g., NSGA-II) that pick one parameter set for the entire batch. These cannot adapt to the \textit{state} of the process -- tool wear accumulates, workpiece geometry changes, and material properties vary between batches.

\subsection{Proposed Approach}
This project develops a \textbf{Digital Twin} of the milling process -- a high-fidelity physics-based simulator grounded in empirical manufacturing literature -- and trains a \textbf{Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)} agent to dynamically optimize cutting parameters pass-by-pass. The agent observes the current state (flank wear, remaining stock, previous parameters) together with the context it is optimizing for (the energy weight and the material), and selects the next pass parameters to maximize a reward that balances throughput and energy while respecting constraints.

\textbf{The headline result is a boundary with a number on it.} Inside a perfect digital twin the classical receding-horizon controller and the learned policy are comparable, and the controller is the better operator as long as the setup can measure the flank wear accurately. A sweep of the regime map puts a number on where that stops being true: once the wear measurement carries a Gaussian error of about \alert{2\% of its range}, the learned policy becomes the better operator. The result is actionable: it says how noisy the setup's own instrument has to be before the learned policy is worth deploying (Section~\ref{sec:regimemap}).

Key innovations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speed-dependent specific cutting energy} $k_c(v_c) = k_{c0} (v_c/v_\text{ref})^m$ creates a genuine trade-off: faster cutting increases energy per unit volume but reduces idle energy.
    \item \textbf{Domain randomization} during training: material/tool coefficients, workpiece geometry, and initial wear are sampled per episode from literature-calibrated ranges.
    \item \textbf{One environment, one policy:} The energy weight $w_2$ and the material context live in the observation, so a single PPO policy covers the whole weight range and a whole distribution of materials.
    \item \textbf{A fair classical baseline:} A part-level controller that maximizes the expected episode reward over the same physics, with the breakage risk, the step penalty and the completion bonus priced.
    \item \textbf{Robustness experiment:} The same two arms on the same scenarios in two regimes: the controller with every advantage, and the controller with a noisy wear measurement.
    \item \textbf{Regime map:} A sweep of the same experiment, the measurement noise on the flank wear, puts a number on the boundary between the two operators: once the wear measurement is noisier than about \alert{2\% of its range}, the learned policy is the better operator, and below it the classical controller is (Section~\ref{sec:regimemap}).
    \item \textbf{Reward sensitivity experiment:} The reward pays the removal rate of every pass, so the credit a plan collects grows with the number of passes it takes, and the measurement shows that part of the gap in the ideal case is an artifact of that accounting (Section~\ref{sec:rateaccounting}).
    \item \textbf{Batch experiment:} A second environment in which one tool cuts a batch of parts, the tool life is shared across the parts and the tool change is priced.
    \item \textbf{Transfer experiment:} Measures how much a policy trained on one material knows about a harder, faster-wearing material.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A Gymnasium-compliant milling environment with explicit physics, energy-aware reward, and domain randomization.
    \item A batch environment in which one tool cuts a batch of parts, the tool life is shared across the parts and the tool change is priced.
    \item A trained PPO policy evaluated via 100-scenario Monte Carlo sweep with 95\% confidence intervals.
    \item Energy-weight ablation study (7 weights $\times$ 3 seeds) mapping the empirical Pareto frontier.
    \item Comparison against 5 classical baselines, all on the same episodes:\\
    Constant-High, Constant-Safe, Rule-Based, NSGA-II Static, Receding-Horizon.
    \item A robustness experiment against the receding-horizon controller in two regimes: the controller with every advantage, and the controller with a noisy wear measurement.
    \item A regime map with one sweep, the measurement noise on the flank wear. It puts a number on the boundary: the learned policy is the better operator once the wear measurement is noisier than about \alert{2\% of its range}, and below it the classical controller is. The two degrade differently: over the noise sweep the controller loses 31\% of its reward and the policy 5\%. Training against the noise does not pay: the noise-trained policy is below the general policy at every noise level.
    \item A batch experiment: the batch context is worth 24\% fewer tool changes and 9.5\% less energy at the same completion, and the per-part controller needs 207.5 s of machine time for a batch of ten parts against the batch-aware policy's 113.1 s.
    \item A reward sensitivity experiment: the reward pays the removal rate of every pass, so the credit a plan collects grows with the number of passes it takes, and the measurement shows that the gap in the ideal case is partly an artifact of that accounting (Section~\ref{sec:rateaccounting}).
    \item Transfer experiment on a harder alloy: a policy trained across a distribution of materials is the best arm there without any adaptation.
    \item A controller initialization experiment whose result reversed: the initialization is now harmful rather than harmless (Section~\ref{sec:controllerinit}).
    \item Interactive Streamlit workstation with 2D stock depletion, micro-topography, G-code generation, and multi-controller comparison.
    \item Full reproducibility: all results regenerated from committed artifacts by\\
    \texttt{experiments/summarize\_results.py}.
\end{enumerate}

Both reference papers treat energy as related work rather than as an optimization target: in each of them energy appears in the discussion of prior art, not among the objectives. Mo et al. optimize the surface roughness and the production time of a multi-pass turning operation and carry the tool condition and the tool damage in the decision variables, so the wear and the roughness marks carry over. Wang et al. (2025a) build their state from sequences of machining parameters and cutting force features, and their objectives from the dimensional error of the machining features and the machining time; neither the tool wear nor the roughness enters their formulation, so only the dynamic parameter optimization carries over. The third paper in \texttt{docs/papers/} (Wang et al., 2025b) solves the discrete step sequencing problem, so continuous cutting parameters are outside its scope. None of the three carries a Taylor wear model, so the wear coefficient of this work is anchored on the tool life that Wang et al. report rather than on a wear equation taken from a paper (Section~\ref{sec:wear}).

The speed-dependent cutting energy model (Section~\ref{sec:physics}) is the key enabler here: without it the energy of a part is fixed by the volume removed, both objectives point the same way, and no weight on the energy term can open a trade-off. Putting the energy weight and the material context in the observation reduces the ablation cost from 21 training runs to one. The receding-horizon controller provides a fair classical benchmark that uses the same physics, re-optimizes at every pass and maximizes the same expected episode reward, and the robustness experiment of Section~\ref{sec:robustness} puts the two on the same scenarios in the two regimes a plant actually runs in. The regime map of Section~\ref{sec:regimemap} then measures where the boundary between the two operators lies, in a sweep of the wear measurement.

\section{Digital Twin Physics}
\label{sec:physics}

All equations are implemented in \texttt{MillingDT/config/physics.py} and used by the environment, baselines, simulator, and evaluation scripts -- a single source of truth. The state the agent sees is the 11-dimensional observation of Section~\ref{sec:obs}: eight physics features followed by the conditioning context, and the context is present in every task, so the equations below are the same equations for every task.

\subsection{Geometry and Kinematics}
Cutter diameter $D = 50\ \text{mm}$, cutting length $L_\text{cut} = 100\ \text{mm}$, total machining allowance $\kappa_\text{total} = 5.0\ \text{mm}$.
\begin{align}
    v_c &= \frac{\pi D n}{1000} \quad &&\text{[m/min]} \label{eq:vc} \\
    \MRR &= v_c \cdot a_p \cdot f_r \quad &&\text{[mm}^3/\text{min]} \label{eq:mrr} \\
    t_\text{cut} &= \frac{L_\text{cut}}{n f_r} \quad &&\text{[min]} \label{eq:tcut} \\
    N_\text{passes} &= \left\lceil \frac{\kappa_\text{total}}{a_p} \right\rceil \quad &&\text{[integer]} \label{eq:npasses}
\end{align}

\subsection{Tool Wear (Extended Taylor Model)}
\label{sec:wear}
Flank wear rate per pass:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \VB = C_\text{wear} \cdot v_c^{\alpha} \cdot f_r^{\beta} \cdot a_p^{\gamma_\text{wear}} \cdot t_\text{cut}
    \label{eq:wear}
\end{equation}
with calibrated coefficients $C_\text{wear}=1.0\times10^{-2}$, $\alpha=0.3$, $\beta=0.2$, $\gamma_\text{wear}=0.15$. Cumulative wear $V_{B,k} = \sum_{i=1}^k \Delta V_{B,i}$.

\paragraph{The coefficient is anchored on a tool life, not on a wear equation.}
None of the three reference papers carries a Taylor wear model, so there is no published wear equation to calibrate the coefficient against. What Wang et al. (2025a) do report is a tool life: 40 parts cut with 5 identical tools, which is about 8 parts per tool. The coefficient is set so that the tool life at the reference cutting condition is about 9 parts per tool, which puts the tool life inside the range where a batch can run out of a tool. The previous value $C_\text{wear}=1.1\times10^{-4}$ gave a tool life of about 870 parts, so the wear constraint did nothing inside a part and the tool life was irrelevant to a batch; the wear model was inert.

\paragraph{The exponents are the exponents of the wear rate, not of the Taylor tool life.}
The classical tool life equation carries the exponents $(4.0, 0.8, 3.2)$, and with them the tool life spans six orders of magnitude over the machine envelope, which is not a usable envelope either. The exponents used here describe how fast the wear accumulates, so the tool life varies only by a factor of a few across the envelope: a faster cut barely shortens the tool life in this model. That is a known weakness of the wear model and it is carried into the limitations of Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. It is the reason the wear constraint acts mainly on the aggregate of a batch rather than on the choice of a single pass.

\subsection{Surface Roughness (Empirical Regression)}
\label{sec:roughness}
\begin{equation}
    \Ra = k_1 f_r^2 + k_2 \VB + \frac{k_3}{v_c}
    \label{eq:roughness}
\end{equation}
with $k_1=12.5$, $k_2=0.8$, $k_3=0.5$. The finished surface (last pass) must satisfy $\Ra \le \RaMax = 1.6\ \mu\text{m}$.

\subsection{Energy Consumption}
\label{sec:energy}
Specific cutting energy grows with cutting speed:
\begin{equation}
    k_c(v_c) = k_{c0} \left( \frac{v_c}{v_\text{ref}} \right)^m,
    \quad P_\text{cut} = k_c(v_c) \cdot \MRR,
    \quad P_\text{total} = P_\text{idle} + P_\text{cut}
    \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
with $P_\text{idle}=500\ \text{W}$, $k_{c0}=2.5\ \text{W}\cdot\text{min/mm}^3$, $m=0.6$, $v_\text{ref}$ from the reference cutting condition.
Energy per pass: $E_\text{pass} = P_\text{total} \cdot t_\text{cut}$.
Total energy per part: $E_\text{part} = \sum_{k=1}^{N_\text{passes}} E_{\text{pass},k}$.

\begin{proposition}[Interior Minimum]
With $m>0$, the energy per part $E_\text{part}(v_c)$ has an interior minimum in $v_c$. The cutting term rises with $v_c$ while the idle term falls, creating a genuine trade-off that the energy weight $w_2$ can select.
\end{proposition}
This is validated by test \texttt{test\_part\_energy\_has\_an\_interior\_minimum\_in\_speed}.

\subsection{Stochastic Tool Breakage}
Probability of catastrophic breakage per pass, applied only once the flank wear passes a threshold:
\begin{equation}
    p_\text{break} = 0.3 \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\VB - 0.15}{\VBMax - 0.15}}_{\text{wear factor}} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{\MRR}{\MRR_\text{max}}\right)^2}_{\text{load factor}}
    \qquad (\VB > 0.15\ \text{mm},\ \text{else } 0)
\end{equation}
where $\MRR_\text{max}$ is the removal rate of the machine envelope at its upper bounds. The draw comes from the environment's own generator, which \texttt{reset(seed=...)} seeds, so a scenario replayed with the same seed breaks in the same place. If triggered, the episode terminates with a $-100$ reward.

\section{Environment Design}

\subsection{Registrations}
Two Gymnasium environments are registered in \texttt{MillingDT/\allowbreak env/\_\_init\_\_.py}. The per-part one is exported as the constant \texttt{MillingDT.env.ENV\_ID} and the batch one as \texttt{MillingDT.env.BATCH\_ENV\_ID}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \texttt{MillingDTEnv-v1}: the part environment. The observation is always $\R^{11}$: the eight physics features followed by the conditioning context (the energy weight $w_2$, the wear coefficient $C_\text{wear}$ and the roughness coefficient $k_1$). The flags \texttt{randomize} and \texttt{randomize\_weight} decide whether the scenario and the energy weight are drawn per \texttt{reset()}. A task with a pinned weight is the same environment with a constant in the context, so there is a single observation shape across the whole project.
    \item \texttt{MillingDTBatchEnv-v1}: the batch environment. One episode is a batch of ten parts cut with one tool. The observation is $\R^{13}$: the eleven features of the part the tool is cutting, then the fraction of the tool life that is left and the fraction of the batch that is still to cut. The tool is replaced when it breaks or reaches its wear limit, the wear of the new tool starts at zero, and the replacement costs 500 reward units against the 200 a finished part is worth. The physics is not duplicated: the batch environment wraps the part environment and adds the tool accounting.
\end{enumerate}

One optional argument makes the environment behave like a plant rather than like a simulator with perfect knowledge. The argument \texttt{obs\_noise} puts noise on the two measured state features, the flank wear and the remaining stock, and it is used by the robustness experiment of Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\subsection{State Space}
\label{sec:obs}
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Observation (11 dimensions), every feature mapped onto $[0,1]$}
\label{tab:obs}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Index} & \textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Normalization} \\
\midrule
0 & Flank wear $\VB$ & $\tanh$ squash over $[0, \VBMax]$ \\
1 & Remaining stock $\kappa_\text{rem}$ & $\tanh$ squash over $[0, \kappa_\text{total}]$ \\
2 & Previous spindle speed $n$ & $(n-n_\text{min})/(n_\text{max}-n_\text{min})$ \\
3 & Previous feed $f_r$ & $(f_r-f_\text{min})/(f_\text{max}-f_\text{min})$ \\
4 & Previous depth $a_p$ & $(a_p-a_{p,\text{min}})/(a_{p,\text{max}}-a_{p,\text{min}})$ \\
5 & Previous $\MRR$ & $\MRR / \MRR_\text{max}$, clipped \\
6 & Previous roughness $\Ra$ & $\tanh$ squash over $[0, \RaMax]$ \\
7 & Tool broken & 1 if broken, else 0 \\
8 & Energy weight $w_2$ & $w_2 \in [0,1]$ \\
9 & Wear coefficient $C_\text{wear}$ & over the scenario band $[0.6, 1.4]$ of nominal \\
10 & Roughness coefficient $k_1$ & over the scenario band $[0.8, 1.2]$ of nominal \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Every feature is normalized against the \emph{scenario's own} limits, so the observation is scale free across geometries and materials. The $\tanh$ squashing keeps the gradient magnitude when a constraint is violated, instead of clipping the value to 1.

The context occupies the last three slots in every task. A policy trained with a pinned weight sees a constant there, a policy trained with the weight drawn sees the weight it is being asked for, and a policy trained across the material distribution sees the material it is cutting. The shape never changes, so one trained policy can be asked for any weight and any material at runtime. The two material slots carry the material in play, so the policy knows which material it is cutting. Of the eight state features only two are measured, the flank wear and the remaining stock, and only those two carry the measurement noise.

\subsection{Action Space}
Continuous 3D box $\mathcal{A} = [-1, 1]^3$, mapped to physical parameters:
\begin{align}
    n &= n_\text{min} + \frac{a_1+1}{2}(n_\text{max}-n_\text{min}) \\
    f_r &= f_\text{min} + \frac{a_2+1}{2}(f_\text{max}-f_\text{min}) \\
    a_p &= a_{p,\text{min}} + \frac{a_3+1}{2}(a_{p,\text{max}}-a_{p,\text{min}})
\end{align}
The policy output is unbounded; the environment clips to $[-1,1]$ before mapping, guaranteeing the machine envelope is never violated.

\subsection{Reward Function}
The per-step reward is:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
r ={}& \underbrace{100\left(w_1 \frac{\MRR}{\MRR_\text{max}} - w_2 \frac{E_\text{pass}}{E_\text{ref}}\right)}_{\text{Objective}}
+ \underbrace{20\,\Delta\!\left(\tfrac{\kappa_\text{removed}}{\kappa_\text{total}}\right)}_{\text{Progress}} \\
& - \underbrace{50\,\mathbb{1}[\VB > \VBSafe]}_{\text{Wear penalty}}
- \underbrace{100\,\mathbb{1}[\text{broken}]}_{\text{Breakage}} \\
& - \underbrace{50\,\mathbb{1}[\text{finishing pass} \wedge \Ra > \RaSafe]}_{\text{Roughness penalty}}
- 0.05
\end{split}
\end{equation}
plus, on a part finished inside spec, a $+200$ completion bonus and a Pareto margin bonus of at most $+5$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{HV} = \max\!\left(0, 1 - \tfrac{T_\text{total}}{T_\text{ref}}\right) \cdot \max\!\left(0, 1 - \tfrac{\Ra}{\RaSafe}\right) \cdot 5
\end{equation}

All of these magnitudes live in the \texttt{reward} section of \texttt{MillingDT/\allowbreak config/\allowbreak physics\_\allowbreak constants.yaml}, so they are in one place and the file states that they are a design decision. \textbf{The scale of the objective term matters as much as the weights.} The objective term is what decides the operating point, so it has to be worth a sensible fraction of the value of a finished part. With the objective scale at 5 the whole throughput and energy trade-off of a part was worth about 1.2 points of reward against a completion bonus of 200, so a policy that finished every part at any cost scored well and the energy weight had almost nothing to select. At 100 the trade-off is worth about fifty points per part, a quarter of the value of a part, and the completion bonus still dominates the decision to finish. The constraint penalties (50 for a pass above the wear safety limit, 100 for a broken tool, 50 for a finishing pass outside the roughness safety limit) sit above the progress bonus of 20, so that breaking a constraint is never worth a throughput or energy gain.

$\RaSafe = 0.85\,\RaMax$, $w_1 + w_2 = 1$, and $E_\text{ref}$ is the energy of a pass under the reference condition, recomputed for every scenario from that scenario's diameter, cutting length and energy coefficients. The reward is validated by \texttt{test\_reward\_matches\_an\_independent\_calculation}, which recomputes it from \texttt{MillingDT/config/physics.py}, independently of the code that assembles it, and asserts agreement to $10^{-9}$.

\subsection{The Reward and the Accounting of a Rate}
\label{sec:rateaccounting}

The reward pays the objective term once per pass, and the objective term carries the removal rate $\MRR$ of that pass. That is the standard way to express a rate in a reward that is paid per pass, and it has a consequence that has to be measured rather than argued about: the total credit a part collects grows with the number of passes the plan takes. A plan that cuts the same part in more, shallower passes is paid more for it.

The measurement was made on the same 100 randomized scenarios at $w_2 = 0.4$ that carry the robustness experiment, by \texttt{experiments/\allowbreak reward\_sensitivity.py}. The script rolls both arms out and accumulates the reward terms in a form that lets the accounting be varied afterwards, so the same rollouts are scored under every objective scale and under every objective form.

The two plans remove the same stock allowance. The controller takes more passes, so it collects \alert{27\% more objective credit} for the same material, and at the objective scale of the configuration that is about nine of the fifteen reward points that separate the two arms in the ideal case. The ideal case comparison is therefore biased against the learned policy, and the bias belongs to the reward, not to either method.

Three things were checked before this was written up as a limitation of the comparison.

\textbf{1. The objective scale is not the lever.} The controller wins at every scale from 5 to 4000, and the gap only grows with it: 7.1 points at 5, 10.9 at 50, 15.3 at 100, 23.9 at 200, 41.2 at 400, 93.0 at 1000 and 352.0 at 4000. The sign never flips. The table is Table~\ref{tab:rewardsens} and the artifact is \texttt{results/\allowbreak evaluation/\allowbreak reward\_sensitivity.csv}.

\textbf{2. The part level alternative was implemented and rejected.} Crediting the material of the part against the energy of the part removes the pass count from the accounting, and under that form the two arms are much closer: a 5.0 point gap at the scale of 100, against 15.3 for the shipped form, and the policy is ahead at the large scales, by 9.4 points at 1000 and 57.4 at 4000. It is not the right fix, because the material a part gives up is fixed by its stock. The volume of a part is $\pi D \kappa_\text{total} L_\text{cut}/1000$, independent of every cutting parameter, so the throughput weight would then be inert: the reward would be an energy objective with a throughput weight that does nothing, and the weight sweep of this project would stop measuring what it claims to measure.

\textbf{3. What a correct formulation would need.} The removal rate of a part is the ratio of a fixed volume to the part's time, and a rate cannot be accumulated over a part without becoming a volume. A reward that expresses it has to either price the cycle time directly, or compare the part's average rate to a reference at the end of the part. Both change the meaning of the weights, and the second makes the objective sparse and the credit assignment poor. Neither was adopted, and the reward as shipped carries the artifact instead, documented rather than hidden.

The consequence for the reading of the ideal case follows from this. The defensible reading of Table~\ref{tab:robustness} is that the two methods are comparable inside a perfect digital twin: the learned policy is ahead on the physical metrics (22\% more material removed, a third less cycle time, 3.7\% less energy) and behind on the tool breakage (6\% against 3\%), and the measured gap on the reward is partly the artifact of how the reward pays for a rate.

\subsection{Episode Logic}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{terminated=True}: Part finished ($\kappa_\text{rem} \le 0$), tool broken, or wear exceeded.
    \item \texttt{truncated=True}: Maximum steps (200) reached.
    \item The roughness penalty is charged once, at the finishing pass, and only when that pass ends outside $\RaSafe$. Intermediate passes carry no roughness penalty; the wear penalty is charged on every pass whose cumulative flank wear exceeds $\VBSafe$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Domain Randomization}
When \texttt{randomize=True}, each \texttt{reset()} samples a new scenario from \texttt{MillingDT/\allowbreak config/\allowbreak scenarios.py}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Workpiece: $\kappa_\text{total} \in [2, 15]\ \text{mm}$, $D \in [40, 150]\ \text{mm}$, $L_\text{cut} \in [50, 200]\ \text{mm}$
    \item Material/Tool: $C_\text{wear}$ at $[0.6, 1.4]\times$ nominal, $k_1, k_2, k_3$ each at $[0.8, 1.2]\times$ nominal
    \item Initial wear: $V_{B,0} \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 0.8]\,\VBMax$
\end{itemize}
The same tables are used for Monte Carlo evaluation, so the policy is measured on the distribution it was trained on. Measured on the trained policy, the eight physics features the policy receives on the two sets agree to 0.041 in the mean of their normalized values, against a standard deviation of 1.

\section{Classical Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

Five non-RL baselines implemented in \texttt{experiments/run\_baselines.py}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Constant-High:} $n=8000, f_r=0.35, a_p=2.5$ (upper bounds).
    \item \textbf{Constant-Safe:} $n=2000, f_r=0.05, a_p=0.1$ (lower bounds).
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based Degradation:} Feed at maximum, spindle speed reduced by 20\% every second pass, as a shop-floor heuristic for tool degradation.
    \item \textbf{NSGA-II Static:} Multi-objective optimization (pymoo) picking \textit{one} parameter set for the whole part. Decision variables $[n, f_r, a_p]$. Objectives: maximize $\MRR$, minimize Energy. Constraints: $\RaFinal \le \RaMax$, $V_{B,\text{final}} \le \VBMax$.
    \item \textbf{Receding-Horizon Controller:} A vectorized grid search over $(n, f_r, a_p)$ that re-solves at every pass, simulating the remaining passes of the part with the current $\VB$ and $\kappa_\text{rem}$. It is a part-level maximizer of the expected episode reward: for every candidate it prices the objective term, the progress bonus, the wear penalty, the step penalty, the completion bonus, the Pareto margin and the breakage probability, and it keeps the candidate with the best expected value. The plan of a part is two parameter sets, a roughing feed and a finishing feed, because the reward penalizes the roughness of the finishing pass only and a plan that holds one feed for the whole part would have to satisfy the roughness limit on the worst pass. This is the strongest classical baseline.
\end{enumerate}

The controller is a baseline in the strict sense: it optimizes the same quantity the environment pays, over the same physics, and it is given the model and the state it is handed. It is not given anything the policy does not have, and it is not a stand-in for the reward. Its quality is the measure of the comparison: on the same scenarios it went from 184.3 to 248.9 when it was changed from a per-pass maximizer of the objective term into a part-level maximizer of the expected episode reward. All baselines use the \textit{same physics} (\texttt{MillingDT/config/physics.py}) and are evaluated on the same 50 episodes of the nominal machining condition, at the same energy weight $w_2 = 0.4$ at which the learned policy is queried.

The claim this work supports is therefore narrow and specific: inside a perfect digital twin the learned policy and the model-based controller are comparable, with the learned policy ahead on the physical metrics and behind on the tool breakage, and the gap that the reward does show is partly the artifact of Section~\ref{sec:rateaccounting}. The advantage of the learned policy appears where the instrumentation is the limit: a noisy wear measurement puts it ahead, 226.8 against 181.3. Section~\ref{sec:regimemap} measures the boundary between the two situations and puts a number on it: the learned policy is the better operator once the wear measurement is noisier than about 2\% of its range. The comparison against a model-based controller is sensitive to how the reward pays for a rate, and that sensitivity is published with the result rather than left out.

\subsection{Controller Initialization Experiment}
\label{sec:controllerinit}

The baseline comparison left one question open: the controller re-solves the next pass with the exact state and the exact physics, so it is a strong teacher, and the learned policy is level with it on the reward. The experiment asks whether a policy that starts from the controller ends up better than the expert it started from.

The method is a behavior cloning step used as an initialization. The controller's behavior is recorded on 2000 randomized episodes, a supervised actor with the same shape as the PPO actor (two hidden layers of 256 with Tanh) is fitted on those trajectories by regression, and the fitted weights are copied into a fresh PPO policy. The reinforcement learning therefore starts from a feasible behavior instead of from noise. The policy is then trained with the same budget as the production policy (1,000,000 steps) and evaluated on the same 100 randomized scenarios at the same energy weight $w_2 = 0.4$. The clone lives in \texttt{MillingDT/\allowbreak agent/\allowbreak controller\_init.py}, the experiment in \texttt{experiments/\allowbreak controller\_experiment.py}, and the trained policy in \texttt{results/\allowbreak run\_PPO\_controller\_init\_seed42/}.

\textbf{The initialization is now actively harmful, and the reason is instructive.} The policy initialized from the controller scores 119.8 against the from-scratch policy's 233.6, a gap of 113.8, which is more than fourteen times the standard error of the mean over the 100 scenarios. The cloned actor starts the policy at the controller's operating point, which is the conservative one: a low feed and a low spindle speed, so that every pass of the part stays inside the roughness safety limit. With the wear constraint now binding, that conservatism is expensive. The policy then learns from a bad starting point, slows down further to stay away from the wear limit, and ends up cutting a part in 42.6 s against the controller's 17.1 s and the from-scratch policy's 11.4 s, while it spends 81.5 kJ per part against 66.4 kJ. It also exceeds the flank wear limit on 9\% of the parts, which the other two arms never do.

\textbf{The earlier revision found the initialization harmless.} It made the policy more consistent and did not change the reward, and the report said so. That was the reward not counting the cost of the slow operating point: a policy that finished every part at any cost looked like a good policy. With the throughput counted, the initialization is what the measurement says it is.

\textbf{So the method to keep from the controller initialization is the record and clone machinery, not the initialization itself.} The recording, the regression and the weight copy are a clean way to put a feasible behaviour into a policy, and they stay in the code, because they are what makes the arms of Table~\ref{tab:controllerinit} comparable. What the experiment now shows is that starting the reinforcement learning at the controller's operating point is the wrong way to use them.

\makecell[l]{Trained on the target\\from scratch (50k steps)} & 101.9 & 26.7\% & 0.0\% & 86.7\% \\
\makecell[l]{Production policy,\\zero-shot} & \alert{190.1} & 100.0\% & 10.0\% & 90.0\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{The production policy is the best arm without a single adaptation step.} It was trained across a distribution of materials with the material context in its observation, so the harder alloy is inside its training distribution. Zero shot it scores the highest reward of the four arms ($+190.1$) and completes 90.0\% of the parts against 86.7\% for the other three. Its worst pass violates the roughness limit in every scenario, and its finished surface in 10.0\% of them, which is worse on that count than the nominal-trained source policy, and it is still ahead on the reward.

\textbf{Adaptation repairs the roughness violations and costs more than it gains.} Fine tuning the source policy for 50,000 steps on the target, or training on the target from scratch for the same budget, both cut the worst-pass violations from every scenario to a quarter of them and take the finished surface back inside the limit, and both land at exactly the same reward, 101.9, against the source policy's 168.5 zero shot. The reason is the material: the target alloy wears the tool twice as fast, and a policy that slows down for that trades away more throughput than it gains in specification. The lesson is that the cost of a material change is not only the roughness, it is the throughput that the repair gives up.

\subsection{Behavior Cloning as an Initialization}
The receding-horizon controller is a competent expert, so it can supply the starting point of the learner instead of leaving the learner to start from noise. The module \texttt{MillingDT/\allowbreak agent/\allowbreak controller\_init.py} records what the controller does, fits a supervised actor of the same shape as the PPO actor on those trajectories by regression, and copies the fitted weights into a fresh PPO policy. The initialization stays a separate concern: the clone knows nothing about the training loop, the copy is a weight assignment between two tensors of the same shape, and the rest of the run is an ordinary PPO training, selected with the \texttt{--init-\allowbreak from-\allowbreak controller} flag. The same separation is what makes the arm comparable: the three arms of Table~\ref{tab:controllerinit} share the environment, the scenarios, the budget and the evaluation code, so the only difference between them is where the weights start. The measurement of Section~\ref{sec:controllerinit} is that the difference matters: the initialization now costs 113.8 reward points, so the machinery is kept and the initialization is not.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What Worked}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The speed-dependent cutting energy model ($k_c \propto v_c^m$) is essential. In an earlier revision of the reward, without it the energy/throughput trade-off vanished and the 21 ablation policies came out identical.
    \item The scale of the reward matters as much as the weights. With the objective term worth about 1.2 points against a completion bonus of 200, the reward did not count the throughput and the policy learned to finish every part at any cost. At about fifty points per part the trade-off is inside the reward, and the operating point of the production policy changed completely: 1608 mm$^3$/min against 1205 before.
    \item Domain randomization + shared scenario tables: the policy generalizes to unseen scenarios (94\% part completion, 4\% finished-surface violations).
    \item Putting the energy weight in the observation reduces the ablation cost from 21 training runs to one. The one policy is ahead of the per-weight policies over the weights from 0.0 to 0.6 and behind at 0.8 and 1.0, so the weight stays a runtime query, but it is not free at the ends of the range.
    \item Training across a distribution of materials removes the need for an adaptation step: on a harder alloy the production policy is the best of the four transfer arms zero shot, while a policy trained on the nominal material has to be repaired.
    \item A controller that maximizes the expected episode reward is a far stronger baseline than one that maximizes the objective term per pass: on the same scenarios it went from 184.3 to 248.9, and building it exposed that the earlier baseline comparison was hollow.
    \item The robustness experiment is the central result of the work: inside a perfect digital twin the two methods are comparable, the learned policy is the better throughput operator, and its advantage appears where the instrumentation is the limit, a noisy wear measurement.
    \item The regime map turns that comparison into a number a plant can act on: the learned policy is the better operator once the wear measurement is noisier than about \alert{2\% of its range}, and the classical controller is the better operator below it. The two degrade differently (a 31\% loss for the controller against 5\% for the policy over the noise sweep), and training against the noise does not pay.
    \item Measuring the reward instead of assuming it found an artifact that a fair-looking comparison would have hidden. The reward pays the removal rate of every pass, so the credit a plan collects grows with the number of passes it takes, and the controller collects 27\% more objective credit for the same material. The ideal case is a tie once that is accounted for, and the sensitivity is published with the result.
    \item The batch context is worth something: 24\% fewer tool changes and 9.5\% less energy at the same completion.
    \item The test suite catches physics regressions (energy interior minimum, cutting energy speed dependence) and reward regressions (golden-value test).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Inside a perfect digital twin the learned policy and the model-based controller are comparable, and the work does not claim a reinforcement learning advantage there. The controller scores 248.9 against the policy's 233.6 (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}) and the gap of 15 is inside the noise, and about nine of those points are the reward's artifact: the controller cuts the part in more passes and is paid for each of them (Section~\ref{sec:rateaccounting}). The learned policy is the better throughput operator (22\% more material per minute, a third less time, 3.7\% less energy) and it pays for that with twice the tool breakage and with more finished surfaces outside the roughness limit. The reading of the ideal case is a tie, not a win.
    \item The crossover at about 2\% of the range of the wear measurement is a property of this controller, this reward and this noise model. It is measured against a controller that uses the reading directly and takes no safety margin for it, and the arm where the controller widens its margin for a noisy instrument was deliberately excluded from the headline comparison, because that is a design choice rather than a training one. The noise itself is Gaussian and applies to the two measured quantities only, so the boundary is a property of this digital twin and not of a plant until the instrument is measured.
    \item \textbf{The comparison is one noise model deep.} The robustness experiment has a single degraded regime, the noisy wear measurement, and the regime map sweeps that noise alone. A controller prepared for a noisy instrument, or a policy trained against a different noise model, would move the crossover, and neither is measured here.
    \item Initializing the policy from the controller is now harmful rather than harmless: 119.8 against the from-scratch policy's 233.6 (Table~\ref{tab:controllerinit}). The clone starts the policy at a conservative operating point, and with the wear constraint binding that conservatism is expensive.
    \item The reward pays a rate per pass, and the credit a part collects therefore grows with the number of passes it takes. This is a property of the reward, not of either method, and it biases the comparison against the policy. The scale of the objective term is not the lever (the sign of the gap never flips from scale 5 to scale 4000), but the form of the accounting is: the part level alternative was implemented and rejected because the material a part gives up is fixed by its stock, which would make the throughput weight inert.
    \item The wear model is anchored on the tool life of one paper and the exponents are the exponents of the wear rate, not the Taylor tool life exponents, so the tool life varies only by a factor of a few across the machine envelope and a faster cut barely shortens it.
    \item The energy weight ablation uses 150,000 steps per weight against 1,000,000 for the production policy, so the ablation frontier is a lower bound on what a pinned policy can reach and may shift with more training.
    \item The reward charges the energy of a pass, while the reported metric is the energy of a part. A part cut in many shallow passes draws less power per pass but spends more time on the idle machine, so the two can move in opposite directions. Both are reported.
    \item Stochastic tool breakage (6\% rate) introduces variance; the reward penalty could be shaped more carefully.
    \item The micro-topography model is a simplified feed-mark cusp; real chatter and vibration are not modeled.
    \item The energy model is the modelling choice of this work, not a result taken from the reference papers, and it is calibrated against a single reference condition.
    \item The frontier is steep at the heavy weights: from $w_2 = 0.5$ to $w_2 = 1.0$ the removal rate falls 41\% for 17\% less energy, and the cycle time rises from 12.7 s to 20.1 s. A decision-maker who needs a large energy reduction pays for it in throughput and in time.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Work}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The sensitivity of the comparison to how the reward pays for a rate:} a reward that prices the cycle time directly, or that compares the part's average rate to a reference at the end of the part, would remove the pass count from the accounting. Both change the meaning of the weights, and the second makes the objective sparse and the credit assignment poor, so neither was adopted. The sensitivity of the published result to this choice has to be measured and published alongside it, as it is here, and the reward as shipped carries the artifact until a better form is found.
    \item \textbf{Against a restricted controller:} the controller re-solves every pass with the exact state and the exact physics, so it is an oracle that a learned policy cannot match from its observation alone. The robustness experiment answers the measurement-noise part of that question; what is left is the information gap inside a perfect twin.
    \item \textbf{The boundary under a prepared controller:} the regime map deliberately excludes the arm where the controller widens its safety margin for a noisy instrument, because that is a design choice rather than a training one. How far that moves the crossover is the next measurement, together with the crossover measured on the noise of a real instrument rather than on a Gaussian model of it.
    \item \textbf{The crossover on a real instrument:} the noise model here is Gaussian and applied to the two measured quantities, so the boundary is a property of this digital twin until the instrument is measured. The same sweep on the noise of a real wear sensor would put the number on a plant.
    \item \textbf{Sim-to-real:} Calibrate physics coefficients on real machine data; add sensor noise and delay to the environment.
    \item \textbf{Multi-objective RL:} Replace scalarization with Pareto-conditioned networks or hypervolume maximization.
    \item \textbf{Runtime energy budget:} Drive the production policy with an optimizer that sets $w_2$ from an energy budget. The weight is a query, not a retrain.
    \item \textbf{Batch-level planning:} let the policy plan a whole batch instead of a part, with the tool life and the tool change in the objective, so that the context of Section~\ref{sec:batch} is optimized rather than merely read.
    \item \textbf{Full 3D Digital Twin:} Extend the 2D cross-section to 3D toolpath visualization with collision checking.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This project demonstrates a complete pipeline for DRL-based CNC milling optimization:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A physics-grounded Digital Twin with speed-dependent cutting energy, tool wear, surface roughness, and stochastic breakage. The wear coefficient is anchored on a tool life rather than on a wear equation, because none of the three reference papers carries one.
    \item Two Gymnasium environments, one for a part and one for a batch of ten parts cut with one tool, with domain randomization, an always 11-dimensional conditioned observation and an energy-aware reward whose objective terms are scaled to a quarter of the value of a part.
    \item PPO policies trained with proper experimental rigor: Monte Carlo evaluation with confidence intervals, an ablation study mapping the empirical Pareto frontier, and one production policy covering the frontier in a single run.
    \item Strong classical baselines including a receding-horizon controller that re-optimizes at every pass and maximizes the expected episode reward over the same physics.
    \item A robustness experiment, the central result of the work: the learned policy and the controller on the same 100 scenarios in two regimes. Inside a perfect digital twin the two are comparable (248.9 against 233.6, a gap that is inside the noise and about nine points of which are the reward's artifact, since the controller cuts the part in 5.85 passes against the policy's 4.00 and is paid for each pass). With a noisy wear measurement the controller falls to 181.3 while the policy holds at 226.8. The learned policy is the better throughput operator in every regime: 22\% more material per minute, a third less time and 3.7\% less energy, at twice the tool breakage.
    \item A regime map with one sweep, the measurement noise on the flank wear, which puts a number on the boundary: the learned policy is the better operator once the wear measurement is noisier than about \alert{2\% of its range}, where the controller loses 31\% of its reward and the policy 5\%, and the classical controller is the better operator below it. The policy trained against the noise is below the general policy at every noise level, including the 5\% it was trained for.
    \item A reward sensitivity experiment that measures the artifact instead of arguing about it: the reward pays the removal rate of every pass, so the credit a plan collects grows with the number of passes it takes, and the controller collects 27\% more objective credit for the same material. The objective scale is not the lever (the gap grows from 7.1 points at scale 5 to 352.0 at scale 4000 and never changes sign), the part level alternative was implemented and rejected because the material a part gives up is fixed by its stock, and the reward as shipped carries the artifact with the sensitivity published next to the result.
    \item A batch experiment: a batch of ten parts cut with one tool, where the tool change costs 500 reward units. The batch context is worth 24\% fewer tool changes and 9.5\% less energy at the same completion, and the per-part controller needs 207.5 s of machine time for the batch against the batch-aware policy's 113.1 s.
    \item A controller initialization experiment, which records the receding-horizon controller on 2000 episodes, clones its actor into a fresh PPO policy and trains it with the same 1,000,000-step budget. The result reversed: the initialization is now harmful (119.8 against the from-scratch policy's 233.6), because the clone starts the policy at the controller's conservative operating point and the wear constraint makes that conservatism expensive. The record and clone machinery stays, the initialization does not.
    \item A transfer experiment on a harder alloy, which finds that a policy trained across a distribution of materials needs no adaptation there, while a policy trained on the nominal material has to be repaired, and that the repair costs more throughput than it gains in specification.
    \item An interactive workstation for visualization, G-code generation, and multi-controller comparison.
    \item Full reproducibility: every result table is generated from committed artifacts by a single script.
\end{enumerate}

The key scientific finding on the physics side is that \textbf{the speed-dependent specific cutting energy creates a genuine energy/throughput trade-off}, and a DRL agent can learn to navigate it pass-by-pass while respecting surface quality and tool life constraints. Across the measured frontier, from $w_2 = 0.5$ to $w_2 = 1.0$, the removal rate falls 41\% while the energy of the part falls 17\%, and the spindle speed falls with the energy weight, which is the mechanism the model predicts. One policy with the weight in its observation makes the whole trade-off accessible at runtime: it is ahead of the 21 policies trained per weight over the weights from 0.0 to 0.6, by 1.4\% to 5.6\% of the removal rate, and behind at 0.8 and 1.0, by 9.0\% and 21.6\%. The weight in the observation is a weaker signal than a dedicated policy where the trade-off is steepest.

The result on the classical side is narrower and more interesting than an advantage, and it is a boundary rather than a claim. \textbf{Inside a perfect digital twin the learned policy and the model-based controller are comparable, and the honest reading is a tie rather than a win for either of them.} The controller computes the optimum of the same objective with strictly more information: it has the exact model, the exact state and the material in its hand. What the learned policy does have is the throughput: it removes 22\% more material per minute, finishes a part in a third less time and spends 3.7\% less energy, and it pays for that with twice the tool breakage and with more finished surfaces outside the roughness limit. Part of the gap the reward does show is not an operating point at all: the reward pays the removal rate of every pass, so a plan that cuts the same part in more, shallower passes is paid more, and the controller takes 5.85 passes against the policy's 4.00. The advantage of the learned policy appears where the instrument is the limit, a noisy wear measurement, and in the batch, where the tool life is a resource shared by the parts. The regime map of Section~\ref{sec:regimemap} puts a number on the first of those situations: the learned policy is the better operator once the wear measurement is noisier than about \alert{2\% of its range}, and the classical controller is the better operator below that, inside a perfect digital twin. The comparison against a model-based controller is sensitive to how the reward pays for a rate, and that sensitivity is published with the result rather than left out, so the comparison is a statement about a design decision as well as about the two operators.

The PPO hyperparameters in Table~\ref{tab:ppo} are the Stable-Baselines3 defaults, with three exceptions: the network is widened to $2\times256$ units, the entropy coefficient is raised to 0.02 to keep the policy from collapsing to deterministic actions early, and the training runs on the CPU with one thread per process so that several policies can train in parallel on one machine. \section{Energy Model Derivation}
\label{app:energy}

The specific cutting energy $k_c(v_c)$ models the empirical observation that the energy required to remove a unit volume of material increases with cutting speed due to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Higher shear zone temperatures $\to$ increased flow stress
    \item Increased strain rates $\to$ strain-rate hardening
    \item Tool-chip interface friction changes
\end{enumerate}

The power-law form $k_c(v_c) = k_{c0} (v_c/v_\text{ref})^m$ with $m \approx 0.6$ is a standard empirical form for the rise of the specific cutting energy with cutting speed, and it is the modelling choice of this work rather than a result taken from either reference paper: the reference papers optimize quality and efficiency, and treat energy only as related work. The reference condition $v_\text{ref}$ is the cutting speed at the calibrated nominal parameters ($n=5000$ RPM, $D=50$ mm $\to v_\text{ref} \approx 785$ m/min). At this speed, $k_c = k_{c0} = 2.5$ W$\cdot$min/mm$^3$.

Total power: $P_\text{total} = P_\text{idle} + k_c(v_c) \cdot \MRR$.
Energy per pass: $E_\text{pass} = P_\text{total} \cdot t_\text{cut}$.
Substituting $t_\text{cut} = L_\text{cut} / (n f_r)$ and $\MRR = v_c a_p f_r$:
\begin{align}
    E_\text{pass} &= P_\text{idle} \frac{L_\text{cut}}{n f_r}
    + k_{c0} \left( \frac{v_c}{v_\text{ref}} \right)^m v_c a_p f_r \frac{L_\text{cut}}{n f_r} \\
    &= \underbrace{P_\text{idle} \frac{L_\text{cut}}{n f_r}}_\text{Idle energy}
    + \underbrace{k_{c0} \frac{L_\text{cut} a_p}{n f_r} \left( \frac{\pi D n}{1000 v_\text{ref}} \right)^m \frac{\pi D n}{1000}}_\text{Cutting energy}
\end{align}

The idle term $\propto 1/(n f_r)$ decreases with speed; the cutting term $\propto n^m$ increases with speed. Their sum has an interior minimum, which is the operating point selected by $w_2$.
